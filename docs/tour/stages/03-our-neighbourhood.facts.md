## Candidate 1
- **Fact:** In 2025 astronomers confirmed Aquarius III, one of the smallest galaxies ever found orbiting the Milky Way — a faint smudge of only ~2,000 stars, 85,000 light-years away, held together mostly by dark matter.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** It has fewer stars than a small town has people, and shows our galaxy's satellite family is still being discovered today.
- **Source:** Cerny et al., *ApJ* 979, 164, 2025 (arXiv:2410.00981). https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/ad8eba
- **Recency:** 2025
- **Confidence:** high — distance, star count, dwarf interpretation confirmed.
- **On screen:** Not shown — an ultra-faint Milky Way satellite, below the tier's magnitude cut, and we're viewing the groups at 4 Mpc. Ties to the BACKLOG "group explorability" gap (faint dwarfs culled at small/medium tier).
- **New feature:** render/label ultra-faint Local Group dwarfs (denser Local-Volume seeding regardless of M_abs). Already a tracked backlog item.

## Candidate 2 — RELEVANT (with a feature)
- **Fact:** The dwarf galaxies orbiting Centaurus A don't swarm randomly as standard cosmology predicts — 14 of its 16 measured satellites move in a coordinated, rotating disk. Fewer than 0.5% of comparable simulated galaxies behave this way.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** The Milky Way and Andromeda each have a similar satellite plane, dismissed as flukes — a third nearby case makes "fluke" hard to defend, openly challenging the dark-matter model.
- **Source:** Müller, Pawlowski, Jerjen, Lelli, *Science* 2018 (arXiv:1802.00081). https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.00081
- **Recency:** 2018 (still actively debated)
- **Confidence:** high — 14-of-16 and <0.5% confirmed from the abstract.
- **On screen:** Partially — the Centaurus A group IS shown (marker + members), so the subject is on screen; the *satellite plane* itself isn't distinguished.
- **New feature:** render Cen A's satellites and highlight their co-rotating disk (a tilted ring of member points). Pairs with denser group seeding; makes a textbook-challenging result visible.

## Candidate 3 (backup)
- **Fact:** The Milky Way and Andromeda sit inside a flat pancake of galaxies called the Local Sheet — 34 million light-years wide but only ~1.5 million thick — ringed by twelve giant galaxies one researcher nicknamed the "Council of Giants."
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** A vivid image (a "council" around our galaxy) for the abstract idea that galaxies arrange into sheets — the cosmic web repeating on our doorstep.
- **Source:** McCall, *MNRAS* 2014 (arXiv:1403.3667). https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2014/03/140311100606.htm
- **Recency:** 2014 (structural-context backup)
- **Confidence:** high — dimensions and the twelve-galaxy ring confirmed.
- **On screen:** Inferable — at this framing the nearby giant galaxies are present and lie in a flattened sheet; if those galaxies are in frame, the flatness reads. The most directly grounded fact at this stage.
- **New feature (optional):** highlight the Local Sheet plane (a faint disk) and label the ring of giants, to make the "pancake" explicit.
