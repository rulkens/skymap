## Candidate 1
- **Fact:** The most detailed 3D map of the universe ever made captured 47 million galaxies and quasars — about 13 million more than planned — because the instrument worked faster than expected.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** People assume surveys hit their target; this one overshot its five-year goal by nearly 40%. (DESI, April 2026.)
- **Source:** "DESI Completes Planned 3D Map of the Universe" — Berkeley Lab News Center, 2026. https://newscenter.lbl.gov/2026/04/15/desi-completes-planned-3d-map-of-the-universe-and-continues-exploring/
- **Recency:** 2026
- **Confidence:** high — official lab press release; figures confirmed by fetch.
- **On screen:** Inferable — the tour *is* a real 3D galaxy map; works as a "what you're flying through is real data" title framing. No single object to show.
- **New feature:** none needed (meta-fact about the data itself).

## Candidate 2
- **Fact:** The observable universe holds at least two trillion galaxies — but more than 90% are too faint and far for any telescope today to see. We've counted only a tenth of what's out there.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** The "2 trillion" headline is semi-known; the punchline that 90% remain undetectable is the part that lands.
- **Source:** "Hubble Reveals Observable Universe Contains 10 Times More Galaxies…" — NASA Science (Conselice et al., ApJ), 2016. https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/hubble-reveals-observable-universe-contains-10-times-more-galaxies-than-previously-thought/
- **Recency:** 2016 (figure still standard)
- **Confidence:** high — NASA release on a peer-reviewed ApJ study; figures confirmed.
- **On screen:** Inferable, but better at a wide stage (05/08/09) where the galaxy field is dense and the "this is a small sample of the real thing" point is visible. Not grounded at the title (only the MW shows).
- **New feature:** none (conceptual scale fact).

## Candidate 3
- **Fact:** In 2024 astronomers found a ring of galaxies 1.3 billion light-years across — so large that, by current theory, structures that big shouldn't exist (the limit is ~1.2 billion ly). The "Big Ring."
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** Few people know there's a theoretical *ceiling* on structure size, or that real discoveries quietly break it.
- **Source:** Lopez, Clowes & Williger, *JCAP* 2024(7):055; via Wikipedia "Big Ring." https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Ring
- **Recency:** 2024
- **Confidence:** medium — traces to a peer-reviewed JCAP paper but retrieved via encyclopedia summary; the "challenges the cosmological principle" framing is contested.
- **On screen:** Not shown — the Big Ring isn't in the tour's catalog or markers. Belongs at a large-scale stage (05) if built.
- **New feature:** add the Big Ring (and its neighbour the Giant Arc) as a featured large-scale-structure overlay — a ring/arc of points or a marker at ~9 Gly. Notable build (new distant structure layer).

## Candidate 4
- **Fact:** An X-ray all-sky telescope cataloged 1,338 superclusters of galaxies — the largest such catalog in X-rays. The heaviest, the Shapley Supercluster, weighs ~26 thousand trillion Suns.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** Superclusters are usually mapped in visible light; doing it in X-rays (the hot gas between galaxies) is newer.
- **Source:** A. Liu et al., *A&A* 2024 (eRASS1; arXiv:2402.08454). https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2024/03/aa48884-23/aa48884-23.html
- **Recency:** 2024
- **Confidence:** medium — figures from the search result; A&A body not separately fetched. "26 thousand trillion Suns" is a rounding of 2.58×10¹⁶.
- **On screen:** Partially — superclusters appear as markers at stages 05–06; the X-ray *hot gas* does not. Meta-fact better at the cosmic-web stage.
- **New feature:** an X-ray hot-gas overlay on supercluster nodes (optional; the MCPM volume already conveys density).
