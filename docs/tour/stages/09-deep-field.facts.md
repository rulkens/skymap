## Candidate 1 — BEST-GROUNDED
- **Fact:** The brightest object ever found in the universe is a quasar, J0529-4351, powered by a black hole 17 billion times the Sun's mass that swallows roughly one entire Sun every day. Its accretion disc — about seven light-years across — shines over 500 trillion times brighter than our Sun.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** It went unnoticed for years because an automated scan of ESA's Gaia data dismissed it as "too bright to be a quasar" and called it a nearby star. Also the fastest-growing black hole known.
- **Source:** ESO (Wolf et al., *Nature Astronomy*), 2024. https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2402/
- **Recency:** 2024
- **Confidence:** high — official ESO release; mass, daily intake, disc size, luminosity confirmed.
- **On screen:** Inferable — the quasars on screen ARE objects like this one; J0529-4351 is literally one of the milliquas points. The shell of quasars grounds it.
- **New feature (optional):** single out and label J0529-4351 within the milliquas set (a "select this quasar" highlight) so "the brightest thing in the universe" has a specific point to look at.

## Candidate 2
- **Fact:** JWST found a feeding supermassive black hole in a galaxy seen just 570 million years after the Big Bang — far too massive for its tiny host and growing faster than theory allows.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** Black-hole mass usually scales with galaxy size; this "little red dot" breaks that, hinting the first black holes outgrew their galaxies.
- **Source:** ESA/Webb (Tripodi et al., *Nature Communications*), 2025. https://esawebb.org/news/weic2522/
- **Recency:** 2025
- **Confidence:** high — official ESA/Webb release.
- **On screen:** Not shown as a distinct object — but conceptually it's one of the distant points; this is a "what these far dots really are" fact rather than something to point at.
- **New feature:** none practical (a single early object). Works as narration tied to the shown quasar population.

## Candidate 3
- **Fact:** A quasar seen ~12 billion years ago (eFEDS J084222.9+001000) hosts a black hole guzzling matter at ~13 times the theoretical "Eddington limit" — the rate at which its own glow should blow incoming gas away — while also firing radio jets and X-rays theory says shouldn't coexist.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** The Eddington limit is supposed to cap black-hole growth; this blows past it tenfold, maybe explaining how black holes got so big so fast.
- **Source:** Subaru Telescope (Obuchi et al., *ApJ*), 2026. https://subarutelescope.org/en/results/2026/01/21/3645.html
- **Recency:** 2026
- **Confidence:** high — official Subaru results page; ~13× Eddington + jet/X-ray combo confirmed.
- **On screen:** Not shown as a distinct object — same as Candidate 2 (a specific quasar among the population shown).
- **New feature:** none practical (single object); narration-grounded by the quasar field.

## Candidate 4
- **Fact:** Some galaxies host "changing-look" quasars that visibly switch on and off — flaring to full brightness then fading to near-dormancy and back — over just a few years, where theory long held this should take hundreds of millions of years. A 2024 study found eight that went dormant then reawakened, some after only 4–7 years.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** A black hole lighting up and going dark in a human lifetime is astronomically a blink.
- **Source:** Wang et al., arXiv:2410.15587, 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.15587
- **Recency:** 2024
- **Confidence:** medium — arXiv preprint (journal version not retrieved); counts from the abstract.
- **On screen:** Not shown — variability over years isn't animated; quasars are static points.
- **New feature:** animate one or more "changing-look" quasars flickering on/off in the milliquas shell — a cheap, eye-catching motion in an otherwise static deep field.
