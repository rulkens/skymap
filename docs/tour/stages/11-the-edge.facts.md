## Candidate 1
- **Fact:** The observable universe is about 93 billion light-years across — a ~46.5-billion-light-year radius — even though the universe is only 13.8 billion years old. The light didn't travel that far; the space it crossed stretched enormously in transit.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** People assume "13.8 billion years old" means "13.8 billion ly in radius." The mismatch is a direct fingerprint of expansion.
- **Source:** Observable universe — Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe
- **Recency:** established physics, cleanly sourced.
- **Confidence:** high — comoving radius and diameter stated in the article.
- **On screen:** Shown — the horizon shell IS this boundary; its size is literally the visual. But it restates the stage narration, so it adds little as a *separate* "did you know."
- **New feature:** none.

## Candidate 2 — BEST "DID YOU KNOW" FOR THIS STAGE
- **Fact:** The cosmic microwave background — the most distant thing we can see, the glow from 380,000 years after the Big Bang — was emitted by matter that sat only about 42 million light-years away at the time. That same matter is now ~46 billion light-years away, carried off by 13.8 billion years of expansion.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** The "edge" of what we can see started out astonishingly *close*; only expansion stretched it across the whole observable universe.
- **Source:** Observable universe — Wikipedia (CMB matter ~42 Mly at decoupling, z ≈ 1091.6). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe
- **Recency:** established physics, cleanly sourced.
- **Confidence:** high — figures stated in the retrieved article.
- **On screen:** Inferable — the horizon shell ≈ the CMB surface (the furthest we can see); the "started close, stretched away" idea is grounded by the shell we're looking at, and it goes *beyond* what the narration already says.
- **New feature (optional):** render the horizon shell explicitly as the CMB surface (a faint mottled microwave texture) to drive the "this glowing shell is the CMB" point home.

## Candidate 3
- **Fact:** In the far future, dark energy will carry every galaxy outside our Local Group beyond a cosmic horizon — redshifting their light away until they vanish. The Milky Way, Andromeda and the rest of the Local Group will remain; the rest of the universe disappears from view.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** The night sky isn't permanent. Future astronomers could find no evidence that trillions of other galaxies ever existed.
- **Source:** Dark energy — Wikipedia. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy
- **Recency:** standard cosmological-constant model.
- **Confidence:** high — stated in the retrieved article.
- **On screen:** Not shown — a future-evolution concept; nothing on the static shell conveys "galaxies will vanish."
- **New feature:** a "fast-forward" mode that animates galaxies receding and fading beyond the horizon, leaving only the Local Group. Striking but a real build (time-evolution of the field).

## Candidate 4
- **Fact:** As soon as JWST switched on it found early galaxies too bright, massive and mature to have formed so soon after the Big Bang — and in 2025 a large spiral galaxy (the "Big Wheel") within the universe's first two billion years, when theory says such a galaxy should be a smooth blob.
- **Why surprising / lesser-known:** A brand-new flagship telescope immediately strained the standard model, and the puzzle is unresolved.
- **Source:** "These 'impossible' galaxies…" — BBC Science Focus, 2025. https://www.sciencefocus.com/space/universe-began-with-a-bang-but-not-the-one-we-thought
- **Recency:** 2025
- **Confidence:** medium — reputable popular-science outlet; primary studies not individually fetched; treat the "too massive" tension as debated.
- **On screen:** Not shown — specific early galaxies aren't rendered, and the horizon shell shows none of them.
- **New feature:** none practical (specific distant objects). Better as narration trivia than a visual beat.
