## Open a card

Click or tap an object and its card is pinned; a search result or a link pins it too. Resting the pointer on an object shows a smaller hover card. Close a card with its cross or with <Keys of="Esc" />. See [Finding things](/docs/guide/finding/#click-and-point-in-the-scene), and for the card on a phone [Screens, quality and domes](/docs/guide/screens-and-domes/#phones-and-tablets).

## What to check before you quote a figure

- **Some figures are fixed.** A spacecraft’s distance and a rover’s position are dated in the row or in the text. Go by that date, whatever the clock says.
- **The planets’ fact sheet names no source.** Check a figure against another source before you quote it; the card’s Wikipedia link is a start.
- **Distance, at the top of a body’s card, is the camera’s.** It changes as you zoom. Read it only on the card of the object the camera is focused on.
- **On a spacecraft’s card, go by the value.** The value of a row says what it measures and when, as in “~480 km above Earth (Sep 2026)”.
- **A zero can mean “not known”.** <Fact id="card-galaxy-no-redshift" inline />
- **“fallback” means a stand-in.** A diameter or an orientation marked that way was filled in by the app.
- **Estimates look like measurements.** The temperature, luminosity and radius of a field star are estimates from two numbers, and the type of a catalogue galaxy is a guess from its colour.
- **Counts have a date and a limit.** <Fact id="card-structure-count" inline /> <Fact id="card-moon-count" inline />
- **No uncertainties.** No card gives an error bar, however many digits a figure has. Round before you quote.

## Units and distances

<Fact id="card-units" />

<Fact id="card-distance-from-sun" />

M⊕ is the mass of Earth, and g is the pull of gravity at Earth’s surface. M☉, L☉ and R☉ are the mass, the light output and the radius of the Sun. Gyr and Myr are billions (thousand millions) and millions of years. Magnitudes run backwards: a smaller number is a brighter object.
