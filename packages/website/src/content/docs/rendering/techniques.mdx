## Galaxies

<Figure id="render-lod" />

The catalogues hold up to about three million galaxies, and from far out nearly all of them are smaller than a pixel. Up close, one galaxy can fill the window, so a galaxy is drawn in three ways.

<Fact id="render-galaxy-fades" />

<Fact id="render-galaxy-disc" />

The pictures are fetched as a galaxy grows on screen, from <Source id="sdss-images">SDSS</Source> or the <Source id="dss">Digitized Sky Survey</Source>; [Size, tilt and shape](/docs/science/#size-tilt-and-shape) says which galaxies carry a photograph of our choosing. A survey picture’s opacity is its own brightness, so its black sky is transparent and the galaxy has no square edge.

## Stars

Stars pose the galaxies’ problem with two additions: there are four times as many, and the camera flies among them, so the ones worth drawing change with every move.

<Fact id="render-star-tree" />

<Fact id="render-star-knee" />

The exposure of the stars also rises as the camera pulls back, by an amount given under [Brightness, exposure and colour](/docs/simplifications/#brightness-exposure-and-colour). ## Planets, moons and rings

<Fact id="render-sphere" />

<Fact id="render-body-light" />

The law is from M. Minnaert, [“The reciprocity principle in lunar photometry”](https://doi.org/10.1086/144279) (1941). It was written for the Moon; we use it for decks of cloud and haze, and leave airless bodies on the plain cosine. Where a body has a normal map, the surface normal is taken from it, so craters and ridges catch the light with no geometry of their own.

<Fact id="render-earth" />

<Fact id="render-rings" />

<Figure id="saturn" />

The formulas are the single-scattering ones of S. Chandrasekhar’s “Radiative Transfer” (1960), as used by L. Dones, J. N. Cuzzi and M. R. Showalter in [“Voyager Photometry of Saturn’s A Ring”](https://doi.org/10.1006/icar.1993.1118) (1993). Ours has a simpler phase function, takes the rings’ density from the map of them, and makes the particles three times as bright as the map’s colour, a factor we set by eye.

## Atmospheres

Sky colour, the red of a sunset and the thin blue line at the edge of a planet all come from sunlight scattered on its way through air. Computing it exactly means integrating along every line of sight, for every pixel, in every frame. [Atmospheres](/docs/science/#atmospheres) says which bodies have such air.

<Figure id="render-limb" />

<Fact id="render-atmosphere-tables" />

E. Bruneton and F. Neyret, [“Precomputed Atmospheric Scattering”](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8659.2008.01245.x) (2008), made this fast: precompute the integrals and keep them in lookup tables, one of them four-dimensional. S. Hillaire, [“A Scalable and Production Ready Sky and Atmosphere Rendering Technique”](https://doi.org/10.1111/cgf.14050) (2020), removed the large table: the sky is recomputed each frame into small ones, and light scattered more than once is treated as isotropic. skymap takes the layout of the first table from Bruneton and Neyret and the rest from Hillaire, and adds the air of other worlds: each body has its own list of up to four gases and hazes. Earth’s numbers are the papers’; the others mix published values, values derived from them and values chosen for the look, and the file marks which is which.

## The ground on Earth and Mars

A map of all Earth sharp enough for a park would not fit in any graphics processor’s memory, and almost none of it would be in view.

<Fact id="render-terrain" />

<Figure id="guide-surface" />

<Fact id="render-terrain-fallback" />

This is virtual texturing: the picture is far larger than memory, and only the parts in view, at the resolution the screen can show, are held. The usual form keeps a page table on the graphics processor. Ours has none: the walk that decides what to fetch also gives each patch its square of the shared [texture](/docs/reference/glossary/#texture). Heights are stored as colours, and a patch’s normal is computed from them per pixel, at the full detail of the height tile.

Which imagery and heights fill which levels, on Earth and on Mars, is under [Imagery and terrain](/docs/data/pipeline/#imagery-and-terrain).

## The cosmic web

The glow of the cosmic web is a cube of densities, described under [Density, filaments and flows](/docs/science/#density-filaments-and-flows).

<Fact id="render-density-march" />

The cube is the <Source id="mcpm-vac">SDSS Cosmic Slime catalogue</Source>’s, made with the [Monte Carlo Physarum Machine](/docs/reference/glossary/#mcpm) of J. N. Burchett and others, [“Revealing the Dark Threads of the Cosmic Web”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.04393) (2020), and O. Elek and others, [“Polyphorm”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.02441) (2020), the <Source id="polyphorm">program</Source> the method was first built into. The app loads the finished cube. The colour ramp is <Source id="colormaps">inferno</Source>. Each pixel’s march is jittered in depth, which trades banding for a fine grain.

The filaments are traced by [DisPerSE](/docs/reference/glossary/#disperse), as [Structures, fields and filaments](/docs/data/pipeline/#structures-fields-and-filaments) describes.

## The black hole

<Fact id="render-lens" />

<Figure id="render-black-hole" />

The approach is that of E. Bruneton, [“Real-time High-Quality Rendering of Non-Rotating Black Holes”](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.08735) (2020), which finds where each bent ray meets the scene from precomputed tables. The code is our own: one table holds the deflection alone, and the disc is found by a short march along each ray. What is computed here and what is invented is under [The black hole at the centre](/docs/science/#the-black-hole-at-the-centre).

## Bloom, tone curves and HDR

[The frame](/docs/rendering/#light-to-screen) says where these run. Bloom follows J. Jimenez, [“Next Generation Post Processing in Call of Duty: Advanced Warfare”](https://www.iryoku.com/next-generation-post-processing-in-call-of-duty-advanced-warfare/) (SIGGRAPH 2014 course), with five sizes where the talk has six, and a threshold that is a setting.

Three of the five tone curves have a source. Reinhard is from E. Reinhard and others, [“Photographic Tone Reproduction for Digital Images”](https://doi.org/10.1145/566654.566575) (2002), in the form with a chosen white point. Asinh is the stretch of R. Lupton and others, [“Preparing Red-Green-Blue Images from CCD Data”](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0312483) (2004). Both papers map a pixel’s luminance and keep its colour; ours maps red, green and blue separately, so a bright pixel also desaturates towards white. ACES is <Source id="narkowicz-aces">K. Narkowicz’s fitted curve</Source>, unchanged.

HDR, for a display that reports a high dynamic range, is a switch under [Make it brighter or calmer](/docs/guide/settings/#make-it-brighter-or-calmer).
