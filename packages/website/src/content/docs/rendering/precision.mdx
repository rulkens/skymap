You can stand in a park in Copenhagen and fly without a cut to where the map of galaxies ends. Two things break on the way: depth, which decides what is in front of what, and position, which begins to jump from one representable number to the next.

<Fact id="render-depth-range" />

The ranges are drawn from the farthest to the nearest, each over the last, so a planet covers the stars behind it without any comparison of depth between the two. Inside the galaxies’ range nothing hides anything, so it has no [depth buffer](/docs/reference/glossary/#depth-buffer) at all: its limits only say where drawing stops. The depth buffer belongs to the bodies, and each body in view clears it and uses the whole of it for itself.

<Fact id="render-reversed-z" />

N. Reed’s [“Visualizing Depth Precision”](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/visualizing-depth-precision/) shows why with pictures. One range that began close to the camera and still held the orbit of Jupiter could not keep the Sun’s edge steady from near Earth; the <Repo path="docs/superpowers/specs/completed/2026-07-20-reversed-z-near0-depth.md">design note</Repo> has the arithmetic.

## Position: subtract first, then shrink

<Fact id="render-double-then-single" />

Earth is about 0.000000000005 [megaparsecs](/docs/reference/glossary/#megaparsec) from the Sun, and so is a camera above it. At that size, neighbouring [32-bit numbers](/docs/reference/glossary/#floating-point-number) are about 13 kilometres apart, so the two positions are the same number or a jump apart. Neighbouring 64-bit numbers are a fortieth of a millimetre apart. The difference of the two, taken in 64 bits, is small enough for 32 bits to hold well, and it is all the graphics processor needs.
