Everything skymap shows is one of three things. **Measured** is what an instrument recorded: a direction on the sky, a brightness, a photograph. **Derived** is computed from measurements by a stated method: a distance from a redshift, a planet’s position from its orbit. **Drawn** is a model or a choice of ours: a ring, a glow, a picture of something no camera has seen. [The science behind skymap](/science/#bins) sorts the whole scene into the three and gives the reasons.

The last column is a first answer, because nearly everything mixes the three. Each section says which part is which.

## Earth and its surface

From orbit the surface is NASA’s <Source id="nasa-blue-marble">Blue Marble</Source> mosaic of August 2004. Come down and sharper satellite and aerial imagery takes over in places, sharpest over Søndermarken, a park in Copenhagen, at 10 centimetres per pixel. The clouds are one fixed composite. The [search](/docs/guide/finding/) can take you down to any of 18 places on Earth.

**Measured:** the surface. **Derived:** where Earth is and which side is lit. **Drawn:** the clouds and the glow of the air. The imagery and its licences are under [Imagery](/science/#sources).

## The Moon

The Moon is a sphere at its true size, going round Earth at an average of 384,400 km, which light covers in about 1.3 seconds.

**Derived:** its position, less closely than a planet’s, because the Moon follows an average orbit while the planets are corrected against NASA’s positions. **Drawn:** its surface map, from Solar System Scope like the planets’.

## Planets and their moons

<Fact id="scene-bodies" inline /> Each is a sphere at its true size, placed where it was or will be at the date and time on the [clock](/docs/guide/time/). Nine have an atmosphere, and only Saturn has rings.

**Derived:** every position, from published orbits. For the eight planets and 18 moons of the giant planets we correct those orbits against NASA’s <Source id="horizons">JPL Horizons</Source> service for the years 1900 to 2100, while Pluto, Charon, the moons of Mars, Puck and Nereid follow average orbits. **Measured:** the surfaces of Pluto, Charon and the larger moons, which are spacecraft photographs. **Drawn:** the atmospheres, and the maps of seven planets, whose maker says the gaps are filled with invented terrain. The orbits’ sources are under [Solar system](/science/#sources).

## Spacecraft

<Fact id="scene-models" inline /> The Voyagers and Hubble move on orbits copied from JPL Horizons in September 2026, and each rover stands where it is now or where its mission ended.

**Derived:** the positions. **Drawn:** the models. The whale and the petunias are not data.

## Stars

Almost every star is one <Source id="gaia">Gaia</Source> measured: those brighter than [magnitude](/docs/reference/glossary/#magnitude) 14, plus <Source id="gcns">Gaia’s stars within 100 parsecs</Source>, with <Source id="hipparcos">Hipparcos</Source> supplying the few too bright for Gaia. The app loads the brightest, up to 12.9 million depending on the data size, and names 118. Each stands where Gaia measured it in 2016, whatever date the clock shows.

<Fact id="scene-constellations" />

**Measured:** a star’s direction, brightness and colour. **Derived:** its distance. **Drawn:** the constellation lines, which you switch on under Labels & Guides in the [Settings](/docs/guide/settings/) panel. The catalogues are under [Stars](/science/#sources).

## The Milky Way

From inside, the Milky Way is the measured stars themselves. Fly out and it becomes a generated model, because every camera we have is inside it. At its centre is the black hole Sagittarius A*, with a glowing disc that we invented, and round it move 40 stars on orbits published after years of tracking them. A blue band along the galaxy’s plane marks the Zone of Avoidance, where its dust hides the galaxies behind.

**Drawn:** the galaxy from outside, the black hole’s picture and the band. **Derived:** the black hole’s distance and the orbits of the stars around it.

## Nearby galaxies

Fly towards a galaxy and it grows from a point into a disc and then into a picture: a photograph we chose for 81 named galaxies, and a survey image for the rest. Among the 81 are the two Magellanic Clouds and Andromeda (M31), which is about 2.5 million light-years away (761 kiloparsecs, plus or minus 11).

**Measured:** the direction, and the photograph. **Derived:** the distance, found without redshift inside 30 [megaparsecs](/docs/reference/glossary/#megaparsec), where a galaxy’s own motion distorts it. **Drawn:** the generated disc a galaxy wears before its picture arrives, and its tilt where no catalogue gives one.

## The galaxy surveys

Every other galaxy is a point from one of four catalogues: <Source id="sdss">SDSS</Source>, <Source id="2mrs">2MRS</Source>, <Source id="glade">GLADE</Source> and <Source id="desi">DESI</Source>. The shape of the cloud they make depends on where each survey looked. <Fact id="survey-gaps" inline />

<Fact id="2mrs-coverage" inline /> DESI is three regions cut from its first data release, off at first. The [quasars](/docs/reference/glossary/#quasar), from <Source id="milliquas">Milliquas</Source>, lie farthest of all.

**Measured:** direction and [redshift](/docs/reference/glossary/#redshift), the stretching of light as space expands. **Derived:** distance, from the redshift and a model of cosmology, and the colour of a point, from two measured brightnesses. How many galaxies load depends on the data size you choose in [Settings](/docs/guide/settings/). The table under [Galaxies and quasars](/science/#sources) counts the objects we draw from each catalogue, and [Known simplifications](/science/#simplifications) says what the points leave out.

## Clusters, superclusters and voids

Named markers stand for the large structures. Most come from two catalogues, of <Source id="mcxc">X-ray clusters</Source> and of <Source id="mscc">superclusters</Source>, and we placed another 42 by hand: 15 clusters, 16 groups of galaxies, 8 superclusters and 3 voids. A void gets a ring with no glow, since it is defined by what is missing. One of the superclusters is Laniakea, which contains the Milky Way and is defined by the flow of galaxies towards its centre.

**Derived:** positions and sizes from the two catalogues. **Drawn:** the 42 placed by hand, and every disc, ring and name.

## Filaments and the cosmic web

The purple and orange glow is the <Source id="mcpm-vac">density of the cosmic web</Source>, computed from the positions of SDSS galaxies by the Monte Carlo Physarum Machine, an algorithm modelled on a slime mould. Two more layers show the same structure differently: filaments traced along it, and flow ribbons that follow the motion galaxies have on top of the expansion.

**Derived:** all three, each the output of an algorithm run on galaxy positions or velocities. The glow is on at first; you switch the other two on in [Settings](/docs/guide/settings/).

## What is not here

<Fact id="scene-not-here" inline /> Nebulae, star clusters and the International Space Station are missing too. A star or a galaxy that the [search](/docs/guide/finding/) cannot find by name may still be there as an unnamed point.

Every named object is listed in the [Object catalogue](/docs/reference/objects/), and [All sources at a glance](/docs/data/) says where each catalogue comes from and how it is licensed.
